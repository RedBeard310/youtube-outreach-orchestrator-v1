The mailbox ceiling opened by a factor of nine, and one extra inbox is not why

Only one inbox went live between Monday and Tuesday. What changed is what the existing ones were busy with. On Monday every live inbox was carrying 4 to 8 follow-ups due that day, which ate its daily allowance before a single new conversation could start. On Tuesday every one of them had zero follow-ups due and 8 free slots. Thirty-seven inboxes times eight is the 296.

That is worth knowing because it cuts both ways. The follow-up load is lumpy, so capacity swings hard from day to day for reasons nobody touched. Tuesday was an unusually open day, and it was spent.

What broke, what's fixed

Yesterday's fix held, in production, on the exact lead it lost

Monday's debrief shipped a fix for an email refused over five bracketed character counts the writer had printed on the ends of its own video titles. The lead was Pacific Cascade Legal, researched, verified address, written email, parked on failed. Tuesday's send picked it back up, stripped the counts, and pushed it at 07:20:45Z. One finished lead recovered, and the guard that catches a genuinely unfinished merge tag is untouched.

The one failure in Tuesday's own run, 5 attempted and 4 sent, was that same lead before the fix reached it.

The enrichment chain's self-update was killing the chain instead of updating it

The enrichment loop watches its own file on disk. When a commit changes it, the loop replaces itself with the new version in place, so a fix deploys without a restart that would kill whatever batch is running. At 17:41:47Z on Tuesday a checkout changed the file, the loop tried to reload, and the log said this and nothing else:

chain.sh: line 201: .../chain.sh: Permission denied

The reload ran the script file directly, which needs the execute bit. That pattern was copied from the campaign loop, which git tracks as executable. The enrichment chain is tracked as not executable, and its service starts it by handing it to bash, so the reload could never have worked. A failed replacement takes the shell down with it, so the one mechanism built to avoid killing the process was the thing killing it. The chain was idle, systemd restarted it 60 seconds later, and nothing was lost. Had a batch been in flight it would have been.

Shipped (1718e78). Both loops now reload by handing the file to bash, which does not care about the execute bit, so losing that bit can never break a deploy again. If the replacement still fails, each one now says so and keeps running on the copy it already has, because a stale chain that keeps enriching beats a dead one.

Verified: both scripts parse clean, and the failure was reproduced on a throwaway script with the same permissions. The old form printed the identical Permission denied and never reached the line after it, which is the proof that the shell is gone. The new form reloaded the same file successfully.

A zero-send day was unreadable, and that's why this one nearly passed as quiet

The grounded metrics said sent_today.total: 4. Nothing in them could tell that apart from four other days: nothing was ready to send, it was a weekend, the send crashed, or a written batch was sitting there waiting for a human. Those need completely different responses and they all looked the same.

Shipped (f278b7d). The gatherer now reads Siege's own plan for the cycle and reports what it held: emails planned, what the ramp allowed, what the mailboxes would take, how many inboxes were live, how many offers couldn't run, how many emails this repo pushed against everything else, and a plain name for whatever actually capped the day. Tuesday's reads awaiting_approval with 150 unpushed. It reads the filesystem only, makes no database call, and a missing or renamed field costs one null instead of the whole report.

Verified: types clean, 14 new test cases pass, including Monday's and Tuesday's real shapes, a deliberate email pause, a renamed field, and no plan at all. Run against the live plan it reports the numbers in the table above.

Running the gatherer twice destroyed its own spend measurement

The OpenRouter burn figure is the gap between two readings of the provider's running total. It wrote a new reading every run and compared against the newest one, so running the gatherer a second time compared against a reading minutes old. That happened during this session: a real $4.61 a day measured over 24 hours became $15.00 a day measured over 72 seconds, and reported runway fell from 40 days to 12. The published numbers had to be repaired by hand, which is the tell.

Shipped (2b2247c). It now compares against the newest reading that is at least six hours old, and won't write a new one until six hours have passed. Six hours sits well inside the daily cadence and well outside an accidental double run. Two back-to-back runs now return the same answer with the reading file untouched.

Recommended next, ranked

  1. Approve Siege's batch, or let it push without approval. This is now the biggest lever in the pipeline by a wide margin, ahead of Brave and ahead of discovery. Tuesday had 150 written emails, 296 open slots and 3,133 eligible leads, and it sent four. The approval gate made sense while the offers were unproven. It is now the single step between a finished batch and a sent one, and it runs on whether somebody happens to be around. Either decide it daily on purpose, or set the ones already trusted to push on their own.
  2. Raise the Brave Search cap, or add a key. Under $3 for one pass at every lead in the paid-search tier. Every day it stays capped, the lane spends 450 lead slots on leads it cannot read. Unchanged for three debriefs and still the cheapest item on any list.
  3. Explain the OpenRouter jump. $1.97 to $4.61 a day, runway 96 days to 40, on a cycle where the pipeline did almost nothing. The finder's log accounts for none of it. The account's activity endpoint needs a management key, so this needs either that key or a spend log in whatever is actually spending.
  4. Decide the discovery pause. Unchanged from the last seven debriefs. Recovery is the only thing filling the parked pool, it is on lap 6, and it is in the part of its book that always needed money. Now that mailbox capacity has opened up, the shape of the constraint has moved: the pipeline is short of nothing except permission to send.
  5. Unblock a few more Siege offers. Nine of sixteen are down, and the reasons are mostly small and named: a subject line in Notion, two follow-up bodies, a batch writer. Not urgent while slots exceed the ramp, but it is what lets a 6,500-lead shelf be worked without emailing anyone twice.
  6. Apify's month didn't turn on the 1st. It is the one recovery route the Brave cap does not touch, it has proven $0.099 per recovered lead, and it has been resting for three weeks on $10 of reserve. Worth finding out what date its cycle actually resets, or lowering the reserve.
The lesson. Three of today's four findings are the same shape: a number that named no cause. Four emails sent looked like a quiet day. One line of Permission denied looked like a permissions problem. Fifteen dollars a day looked like a spending problem. In each case the system already held the answer, in a plan file, in a mode bit, in a timestamp, and what reached a person first was a figure with the cause stripped out. The fixes shipped today do not send more email or spend less money. They make the next zero-send day say why.

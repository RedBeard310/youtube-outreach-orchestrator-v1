Then 60 planned emails did not go. Three different shapes, one cause. 53 leads were refused one at a time by SmartLead. 4 batches exited having pushed nothing, 3 of them dying at the very first call they make, before any lead was attempted. 7 leads sat inside those dead batches and were never tried. 69 and 53 and 7 is 129, which accounts for the whole plan.

None of those 60 people are lost. A failed push rolls its own record back and leaves the lead where it was, so Siege re-plans them. What was lost is a day of volume, on a bank that four delivery days a week draw about 800 emails from and almost nothing refills.

What broke, what's fixed

1. The SmartLead account rate limit, which nothing in the pipeline waits for

Fixed: youtube-email-outreach-v1 eedbbff87. One thing for Casey to check, below.

SmartLead's limit is 200 requests a minute for the whole account. Not per campaign, not per caller. Two things were spending it at once.

The push spends a handful of requests per email. The reply poller spends far more: it scans 71 campaigns in about 43 seconds and runs every two minutes, which is roughly 99 requests a minute in bursts. It ran nine times inside the 17-minute push window, and found 0 new replies on every one of those nine runs. So the push was sharing a 200-request minute with a job using about half of it to learn nothing.

Three leads were refused with an outright 429 Too Many Requests, quoting the 200-a-minute rule by name. Three batches died on listCampaignMailboxes, which push-batch calls before its first lead to learn which names an email is allowed to sign off as, and a 429 there takes the whole batch and every lead in it. One campaign was left holding a lead it could not be started with, which escalated correctly.

Nothing retried any of it. A refusal that lasts a few seconds became a lead that waits a day. The two consumers are separate processes on separate timers in separate repos, so they cannot be coordinated in code; the push runs as about 90 short-lived processes, which means an in-process rate limiter would govern nothing. Backing off on the refusal is the one thing that works across that boundary.

So every SmartLead call now waits out a 429 and asks again, honouring SmartLead's own Retry-After and otherwise waiting 8, then 20, then 45 seconds, capped. Reads also retry a server error. Writes never do, because a 500 on the add-lead call might mean the email is already queued, and mailing somebody twice is the one mistake this pipeline must not make.

Verified: both typechecks clean, all 273 existing tests pass, and 6 new tests drive each branch of the policy against a stubbed network, so no request left the machine and nothing was sent.

2. The other 50, where SmartLead answered 200 and imported nobody

Instrumented, not yet explained: same commit. This is the one open question of the day.

Of the 53 per-lead refusals, only 3 were an honest 429. The other 50 came back as a success: HTTP 200, with SmartLead's own counter saying it had imported zero leads and naming no reason. The push catches that and fails the lead, correctly, because an email that was never imported will never be sent.

Why it did that is unknown, and it was unknowable: the reply that would have answered was thrown away one line after it arrived, so all anybody could read was the words "no reason given", fifty times. Those 50 cluster in the back half of the push alongside the proven 429s, which makes a throttle the obvious suspect, and suspicion is as far as the evidence goes. It could also be the duplicate rule, since a lead already sitting in another campaign is declined by default and these leads are now being worked through a second and third offer.

So the refusal now prints SmartLead's whole reply when it names no reason, and the log line that carries it was widened from 160 characters to 500 so the reply survives the printing. One cycle from now this stops being a mystery.

What Casey should know. There is a small duplicate-send risk hiding in those 50, and it cannot be ruled out until that reply is read. The push records the send in Postgres first and then pushes, and it deletes its own record when the push fails. If SmartLead actually did import any of those 50 while reporting zero, we deleted the record of an email it will send, and the lead goes back in the queue to be written again. Nothing proves that happened. It is worth one look at whether any of those 50 addresses appear in a SmartLead campaign.

3. "partial_push" named a 60-email hole and could not say what made it

Fixed: youtube-outreach-orchestrator-v1 8c50b1d. Nothing for Casey to do.

The snapshot this report is written from said binding_constraint: partial_push and unpushed_planned: 60, and stopped there. That label reads as a soft shortfall, a few leads a writer could not finish. It covered a rate limit, three dead batches and seven leads nobody ever tried, which are different faults with different fixes, and telling them apart meant reading another repo's log by hand and counting. That is the manual step the last four debriefs have each been about removing.

send_plan.push_outcome now carries the push's own tally: sent, failed, how many batches died before their first lead, how many escalations, and the top refusal reasons with a lead count each. Same rules as the rest of that file: filesystem only, no import from the other repo, an unreadable log reports "not measured" rather than zeros, and campaign and lead ids are generalised out of the reason text so 53 identical refusals count as one row instead of 53.

Verified against this cycle's live log: 69 sent, 53 failed, 50 silent and 3 outright 429s, matching a hand count line for line. One of the six new test cases pins the bug that first made it read 54: the batch writers print a FAIL of their own for a lead whose copy failed checking, and that lead was never pushed at all.

4. Yesterday's two Brave fixes, both confirmed on live data

No new code. Recorded because a fix nobody checks is a guess.

The website search really is reaching the lane. The no-site rate went 67.5% to 16%, and the new per-pass line printed on all three passes: 24, 24 and 30 searches, 19, 18 and 25 of them resolving a site, 0 key refusals and 0 errors. Yesterday none of that existed and three alarms could only say unexplained.

And the spend estimate in that report was about five times too high. It projected roughly 405 searches a cycle, near $2 a day, by assuming every walked lead needs a search. Only the ones with no site already on file do, which is 24 to 30 of 150. 78 searches, under 40 cents. Brave is not a cost worth a decision.

One loose end for the next cycle, small. In the newest pass the two measures do not reconcile: 24 of 150 leads finished with no website, while the Brave line reports 30 searches with only 5 coming back empty. They are counting slightly different things, and it is a question about the instrument rather than a fault in the lane.

Recommended next, ranked

  1. Keep the reply poller out of the push's minute. This is the cheapest lever on the board and it is the only one that needs a human, because the poller lives in automator, which the autopilot does not edit. It scans 71 campaigns every two minutes and found 0 new replies on all nine runs inside yesterday's push. Either lengthen its interval, or have it stand down while siege-plan.service is running. The retries now shipped make the push survive the collision; they do not make the collision stop costing time.
  2. Read tomorrow's push_outcome before anything else. It is the whole verdict on today's work, in one block of the snapshot. If reasons is empty, the retries closed it. If the 50 are still there, SmartLead's own reply is printed next to them now and will name the cause instead of saying "no reason given".
  3. Have somebody check whether any of those 50 leads reached a campaign. Small, and worth it because it is the money path. If SmartLead imported a lead while reporting it had not, we deleted our record of an email it will send and the lead is queued to be written again. Nothing proves that happened; the cost of looking is one query against a SmartLead campaign list.
  4. Decide what October's recovery budget is. Held at number four only because this cycle produced louder problems. The $100 Apify allowance went in two days and bought 472 leads at thirteen cents each. Doing nothing is a real answer, and it now has a sharper price: the 6,965 left to write shrink by roughly 800 a week, and this cycle proved the free lane cannot slow that down.
  5. Unblock a few Siege offers. Nine of sixteen, same named reasons for nine debriefs. With 6,965 researched leads and 35 inboxes, the variety of message we can send is the live ceiling and the supply of people is not.
  6. Decide the discovery pause. Thirteenth debrief carrying it. The work it was meant to fund, enriching what we already have, has been finished at 99.9% for four days, and the lane it left running walked at full strength this cycle and recovered nobody.

Today the search is on, 126 of 150 leads had a website to work from, and 3 of them gave anything up. That is not a fault anymore, it is an answer, and it moves the open question from "why is the lane quiet" to "what refills the bank".

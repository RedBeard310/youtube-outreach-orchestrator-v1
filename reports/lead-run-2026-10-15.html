The three questions

Q1782 yesterday, 64 today. Is that just the follow-up bill coming due?

Half of it. The other half is that you have 14 fewer mailboxes than you did on Monday. The follow-up story is the one yesterday's debrief told, and it checks out: 562 follow-ups came due against a 645-message daily allowance, which leaves 67 slots for new people, and 24 of the 43 live mailboxes had no room at all. Every offer is three emails on days 0, 4 and 10, so a 782-person day on Tuesday books three messages each for the rest of the week.

But the allowance itself fell, and that is the part nothing was watching. Across three mornings the plan read 57 live mailboxes and 855 messages a day, then 53 and 795, then 43 and 645. A quarter of the fleet and 210 messages a day, gone in three days, and each of those numbers looked ordinary on its own morning because nothing compared them to the one before.

The cause is in the email repo, on disk, and it is unambiguous. The inbox health gate runs once a day before the push. On 10-13 it suspended 4 mailboxes. On 10-14, 83 minutes before the push, it suspended 13 and resumed 2, its second-biggest pull in 50 recorded runs. Not one was suspended for being too young in its warmup. All 13 failed on health score, with readings of 53.5, 65.5, 66.7, 68.2, 68.5, 68.5, 79, 80.5, 81.2, 83.9, 83.9, 88.4 and 89.5 against a bar of 90.

Said carefully, because this is a correlation and not a proof. The gate also suspended 6 mailboxes on 10-11 after a weekend with no sends at all, so suspensions churn for reasons that have nothing to do with volume. What I can show is the timing and the size: the largest push you have ever run, and the next morning the largest health pull in five weeks. That is a question worth asking the same morning it happens, which is now possible.

Health score comes from InboxKit and is a composite of deliverability signals, so I cannot take it apart from here and name which signal moved. The gate's rule is public and simple: pause below 90, below warmup day 14, or below a 90% landing rate with real volume. 51 mailboxes are paused by the gate right now, across every cause and every date back to July.

Q2Did the push itself lose anything?

Nothing to SmartLead, and the one fix that was tested held. Zero emails failed. SmartLead's account-wide limit of 200 requests a minute refused listCampaignMailboxes twice, at 10:43Z and 10:53Z, and the retry added on 10-05 absorbed both on the second attempt. The sequence-call retry shipped yesterday was not needed today and cost nothing to carry.

10 planned emails never reached SmartLead, and 9 of them went to the same cause. The push refused them with not in the Siege manifest: the batch's lead list named a person the manifest file has no address entry for, so the push drops them rather than guessing which address to use. That refusal is correct. The disagreement between the two files it is reporting is not.

It is rare but it arrives in clumps, and one clump was expensive. Across the whole send log this refusal fires on three days only: 6 leads on 09-28, 131 leads on 10-07 against 336 sent that day, and 9 today. Every other push day is zero. So this is not background noise, it is an intermittent fault in the file-writing step, and on its worst day it threw away 28% of a push. Both files are written by automator, which this agent may not edit.

The tenth refusal was ours on purpose: the writer's own length gate rejected a draft after its retries, at 181 words against a 206 to 266 window and 77 words above the closing block against 105 to 162. The snapshot splits that one draft into its two failing checks, which is why its reason list adds to 10 while the log shows 9 manifest misses and 1 gate rejection.

Q3The recovery lane's biggest error cause held 60% of the pass. Is something broken?

Not the thing the snapshot pointed at, and yes, something else was. The top cause read 34-channel-page: page rendered with no outbound links, 51 of 85 errors. That message is the method saying the creator genuinely has no links to follow: it checks for the rendered channel markers first and says something different when the page did not render. It can hold most of a pass with nothing wrong, and the snapshot's own guidance says a cause holding most of a pass is one fault to chase. So it pointed at a non-fault for three days.

The real fault was the second row, and it had been climbing all cycle. 17-podcast: iTunes N went 12, then 69, then 56, then 28 across the cycle's four passes: 165 of 552 leads never got that method at all. The cause is now measured, not guessed. Apple meters its podcast search per address, the collect pass asks it 8 leads at a time, and a reproduction from this box got 20 refusals out of 40 calls, mixing 403 and 429. The same 40 one at a time, at a human pace, all answered.

Why nobody could read that for a day and a half: both error condensers collapse every digit to N on purpose, so a rate limit and a dead endpoint print the same word. The status code is the one number in an HTTP failure worth keeping, and it was the one number being deleted. That is also how 18-rdap hid a Cloudflare 403 for weeks before the 10-12 fix.

Three things shipped for this, all verified: the search now queues behind a window that widens when Apple refuses and narrows when it answers, gives up once for the pass when the budget is really gone, and every HTTP failure in the lane carries a word beside its number, so a future iTunes N reads iTunes N rate-limited. Separately, the snapshot now names the largest cause that is not a deliberate content outcome.

The thing worth your attention

The binding constraint moved from the shelf to the fleet, and only one lane can refill the shelf at scale

For sixteen debriefs running, the question has been what refills the shelf. That question just changed shape, because the shelf's runway now depends entirely on how fast you are allowed to send.

At last week's actual pace the shelf lasts about two and a half weeks. The five sending days from 10-08 to 10-14 loaded 2,095 emails, and 5,135 people remain who have never been written to. At today's fleet it lasts about nineteen weeks, because 43 mailboxes owing 562 follow-ups a day leave room for roughly 67 new people, which is about 270 a week. Neither number is a plan. The gap between them is the whole decision: a healthy fleet drains the shelf in a month, and a crippled one gives you five months of runway you do not want.

And the arithmetic on refilling is now measured, which it was not before. October's Apify allowance bought 472 people into the shelf for $89.93, about 19 cents each, across 13 runs on the 1st and 2nd of the month. The free recovery lane, running four passes a day on a book it has now walked ten times, delivered 8 addresses and 4 parks today. On the days it ran, the paid lane parked 236 people a day against the free lane's 4, so about sixty times, and it is dry until 31 October because the whole month's $100 went in the first two days.

So the two levers are priced. Refilling 5,000 people at 19 cents is about $950. Keeping the fleet healthy costs nothing but restraint in how a push is spread. Both are your call, and the second one is free.

The 2.5-week figure uses the real trailing week, which includes the 782-day that may itself have caused the suspensions, so treat it as the fast end rather than the normal. Yesterday's debrief said 4.9 weeks using the fleet's allowance arithmetic; the week actually ran at double that allowance, which is part of why I am writing about the mailboxes today.

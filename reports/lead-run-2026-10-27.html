The three questions

Q1Did the healthy held mailboxes come back, and did capacity go up?

They came back and capacity did not go up. Six returned to normal during the day. One is still there. The other five were stopped again within hours, and every one of them was stopped for the same reason: warmup emails landing in spam folders. The numbers, scored out of 100 by the mailbox provider:

casey.brown@askcontentgetsclients.com cleared at 09:05Z at 90 or better and was stopped at 04:05Z reading 55.0, with 4 of its last 29 warmup emails in spam. caseybrown@askcontentgetsclients.com: 3 of 28, down to 64.4. caseybrown@startcontentgetsclients.com cleared at 15:05Z and was stopped at 18:05Z, three hours later, at 48.7 with 5 of 46 in spam. Two more followed the same path.

Compare that with the thirteen mailboxes that have stayed clear all along. Every single one of them has zero spam complaints across roughly 55 warmup emails. Those two groups are not the same fleet having a good and a bad day. They are a clean cohort and a sick one, and the recovery rule keeps handing the sick one back its sending privileges.

Here is the mechanism, and it is worth your attention because it means the rule cannot work as written. A mailbox gets held when its score drops, and the score is driven by how many of its last three days of emails landed in spam. To come back it needs 48 unbroken hours at 90 or better. A held mailbox sends less, so its three-day window empties out on its own, its score climbs, and it clears. The clock is testing a quiet mailbox, then putting a working one back in traffic. The first few sends answer the question the clock could not, which is why five of six failed inside a day.

A second instrument agrees. The mailbox provider cut warmup volume in half on 23 mailboxes today for a critical spam rate, against 14 yesterday. This is a deliverability problem spreading across the fleet, not a scoring quirk. None of it lives in the five pipeline repos: the rule is scripts/inbox-health.py in automator and both of its numbers are config.

Q2What did the day actually send, and did it reach anyone new?

It sent cleanly and it did reach new people. 61 emails went out and nobody was lost. Siege planned 60 and sent 60. Eight emails were skipped, and the skips cost nothing: five of the eight were written again in another batch and sent in the same run, and the other three were never in the plan to begin with. One more email came from the orchestrator's own send path.

Three of the 58 people emailed had never heard from us before, which is 5.2%. Across the previous seven days, 680 people got an email and only 10 were new, which is 1.5%. One of the three came off the enriched shelf, and that moved a count which had not budged in ten days: people already written to went 1,666 to 1,667.

The ceiling is still the fleet, and it is explicit in the plan. The warmup ramp would allow 870 emails. The live inboxes would accept 60. The smaller number wins, and binding_constraint says so by name: mailbox_slots. 1,730 ready people are waiting for a slot, 672 of them in the top tier. Follow-ups already owed 353 of the fleet's capacity before a single new email was considered.

The recovery lane found 11 contact points and 1 email address from 6 people, against 82 points and 18 addresses over the prior week. That is a picked-over book rather than a fault: the newest judged pass walked 150 leads, found 656 contact methods, and 651 of them were already on file. It is lap 12 of a 2,398-lead book, 100% re-walk, 3.3% of leads with no website, cause: resolution_ok, zero search refusals, and stranded held at 3.

The new cost-per-lead figure held steady on its second day: $0.1684 against $0.1654, a move of under 2%. The field it replaced swung between $0.32 and $3.74 on nothing. The YouTube key pool reads 15 working of 66, which is settled and inert, and finding new channels is off by your 2026-09-08 order.

The thing worth your attention

The fleet is not recovering. It is cycling sick mailboxes through the clear band and back out, and each lap ends with more of them stopped.

Across seven mornings the clear count has stopped falling, at 13, but the stopped band has climbed from 25 to 39. Eight more held mailboxes are healthy right now and queued to clear, one of them within the hour. On today's evidence, most of those will be handed back their sending privileges, send a handful of emails, pick up two spam complaints and be stopped again, because the test they passed measured them while they were idle. Meanwhile 23 mailboxes had their warmup volume cut in half today for a critical spam rate, against 14 yesterday, so the underlying deliverability is getting worse rather than better. The thirteen clean mailboxes are genuinely clean, with zero complaints across about 55 emails each. The question is no longer when capacity comes back. It is whether the 45 held mailboxes are recoverable at all, or whether the answer is new domains.

Recommended next, ranked

  1. Treat the 45 held mailboxes as a deliverability problem, not a waiting problem. Today proved the timer does not fix them: six cleared, five failed again within hours, and the provider cut warmup volume on 23 mailboxes for a critical spam rate against 14 the day before. The thirteen clean mailboxes have zero spam complaints across about 55 emails each, so the fleet has split cleanly in two. The decision in front of you is whether the sick 45 are worth recovering at all, or whether the money goes into new domains and a fresh warmup. I can measure either but I cannot make that call.
  2. If you do keep the recovery rule, make it prove itself under load. The rule's flaw is specific and small: it reads a three-day spam window on a mailbox that has been sidelined, so idleness itself produces the passing score. A version that cannot be fooled would require the clean 48 hours to include real sending volume, or would return a mailbox at a fraction of its allowance for a few days before giving it full slots. Both are edits to automator/config/inbox-health.json and scripts/inbox-health.py, outside the five repos I touch, and both trade a slower return for a return that holds.
  3. The first-touch rate is the one number that improved, and it is worth protecting. Three new people in a day against ten in the prior week, and the enriched shelf moved for the first time in ten days. That happened on 60 emails, which means the ratio got better rather than the volume. 1,730 ready people are waiting for a slot and 672 of them are top tier. When capacity returns, the thing to check is whether those slots go to new people or back into repeat offers.
  4. Decide the branded-shortener question, since it is now the whole remainder. After yesterday's fix the leftover redirect faults are 55 of 152 and they sit on roughly 14 leads. Those are links the creator published that point at the creator's own site, and the lane refuses all of them, so those leads yield nothing on every lap forever. The safe version requires the destination to pass the same ownership test a search result already passes. This is a data-quality call, so it stays with you.
  5. Nothing to do about enrichment or the recovery lane. Enrichment ran zero batches because ten leads remain unenriched out of 6,823. The recovery lane found 11 points on a book it has walked twelve times. Both are correct behaviour for a pipeline with discovery switched off: the only inflow is the weekly subscriber-floor sweep and the one or two addresses a day this lane recovers.
